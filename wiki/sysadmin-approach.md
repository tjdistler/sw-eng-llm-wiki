# Sysadmin Approach

**Summary**: The industry-standard model for running complex computing systems: assemble existing components, deploy them, and staff a separate operations team to keep them running. The [[sre-discipline|SRE]] book opens by cataloguing why this model has structural problems that worsen as the system grows.

**Sources**: `raw/site-reliability-engineering/chapter-01-introduction.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-04-17

---

## The model

Historically, companies have employed systems administrators to run complex computing systems. The sysadmin approach involves assembling existing software components and deploying them to work together to produce a service; sysadmins are then tasked with running the service and responding to events and updates as they occur (source: chapter-01-introduction.md). Because the sysadmin role requires a markedly different skill set from that of a product's developers, the two functions are separated into discrete teams — "development" and "operations" (or "ops").

## Advantages

The sysadmin model is attractive because it is easy to adopt (source: chapter-01-introduction.md):

- It is a **familiar industry paradigm** — many examples to learn from and emulate.
- A relevant **talent pool is already widely available**.
- **Off-the-shelf tools, software components, and integration companies** exist to help run assembled systems, so a novice ops team does not have to reinvent the wheel.

## Direct costs

Running a service with a team that relies on manual intervention for both change management and event handling becomes expensive as the service and its traffic grow. **The size of the team scales with the load generated by the system** (source: chapter-01-introduction.md). This is the linear-scaling problem SRE is designed to avoid.

## Indirect costs: the structural conflict

The indirect costs are subtler but often more expensive than the direct ones. Two teams with different backgrounds, skill sets, and incentives (source: chapter-01-introduction.md):

- Use **different vocabulary** to describe situations.
- Hold **different assumptions about risk** and about what is technically possible.
- Hold **different assumptions about the target level of product stability**.

Over time, the split stops being about incentives and becomes one of communication, goals, and eventually trust and respect. Treynor Sloss calls this **a pathology**.

### The tension expressed

Stated in the most direct terms:

- Dev: *We want to launch anything, any time, without hindrance.*
- Ops: *We don't want to ever change anything in the system once it works.*

Neither group will state their interests this baldly. Instead, they resort to trench warfare (source: chapter-01-introduction.md):

- **Ops introduces launch and change gates.** A launch review may contain an explicit check for every problem that has ever caused an outage in the past — an arbitrarily long list with not all elements providing equal value.
- **Dev responds by reframing what counts as a launch.** They have fewer "launches" and more "flag flips", "incremental updates", or "cherrypicks". They shard the product so that fewer features are subject to the review.

The result is a lot of motion that evades the gates rather than improving the system.

## Why most outages are caused by change

Most outages are caused by some kind of change — a new configuration, a new feature launch, or a new type of user traffic. That is the proximate reason the two teams' goals are fundamentally in tension (source: chapter-01-introduction.md). SRE does not deny this fact; it reframes change itself as something to be made safer through [[change-management-sre|automation]] and [[progressive-delivery|progressive delivery]], rather than something to be gated against.

## What SRE substitutes

SRE's move is to eliminate the dev/ops personnel split: the operations function is staffed by software engineers, who share background and vocabulary with the product developers and can replace manual work with automation. The structural conflict is resolved by the [[error-budget]] — a quantified, shared currency for trading reliability against velocity. See [[sre-discipline]] and [[devops-vs-sre]].
