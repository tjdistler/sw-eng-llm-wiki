# Breaking Changes

**Summary**: A change to a microservice that breaks an existing consumer's expectations — either structurally (schema) or semantically (behaviour). Newman treats eliminating accidental breakages and managing deliberate ones as a survival requirement: organisations that don't sort this out don't last long enough to become large microservice organisations.

**Sources**: `raw/monolith-to-microservices/chapter-05-growing-pains.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-04-16

---

## The contract is implicit whether you like it or not

A microservice's interface is a contract — not just "the data I'll return" but the *expected behaviour* of the service (source: chapter-05-growing-pains.md). The contract exists whether or not you've made it explicit. Breaking it breaks consumers.

[[independent-deployability]] requires that you can change a microservice and deploy it without forcing redeploys elsewhere. That requires *not* breaking your consumers' expectations on every release.

## How breaking changes show themselves

Two warning signs (source: chapter-05-growing-pains.md):

1. **Production outages immediately after a release** caused by incompatible changes to a service that other services consume. Bad — and worse without a fast rollback. The one upside: these failures normally show up quickly, unless the broken part of the contract is rarely exercised.
2. **Lock-step releases**: orchestrating simultaneous deployment of multiple services because their contracts don't line up. Occasional lock-step inside a team is tolerable. Frequent lock-step is a smell — investigate.

Newman observes this is an *early* growing pain, especially when development is spread across more than one team. Within a single team, awareness is higher because the same developers often write both sides of the contract.

## Two kinds of breakage

(source: chapter-05-growing-pains.md)

- **Structural**: shape changes — `calculate(int, int)` becomes `calculate(int)`. Visible in a schema diff if you have a schema.
- **Semantic**: shape unchanged but meaning changed — `calculate(int, int)` used to add the integers, now multiplies them. Invisible to schema tools; only catchable through testing.

## Newman's three rules

The serious version of his "don't break contracts; see rule 1" joke (source: chapter-05-growing-pains.md):

1. **Eliminate accidental breaking changes.**
2. **Think twice before making a deliberate breaking change** — can you avoid it?
3. **If you must break, give consumers time to migrate.**

### 1. Eliminate accidental breaking changes

- **Use an explicit schema** (source: chapter-05-growing-pains.md). Even if developers complain about the friction, schemas make structural breakage obvious. Tools like `protolock` can prohibit incompatible Protobuf changes.
- **Make schema-as-code edits an explicit step.** If a developer must hand-edit the schema, they pause. If the schema is autogenerated by reflection, they don't.
- **Avoid technology that magically serializes data or generates schemas from code** when external contracts are at stake. Hand-rolling makes the cost of contract change visible.
- **For semantic breakages, testing is the answer.** Especially [[consumer-driven-contracts]] (Newman's recommended technique for catching consumer-affecting changes from the consumer's perspective).

### 2. Prefer expansion over change

Add new methods, resources, topics, or fields that support the new behaviour while keeping the old. You may have to maintain old code longer, but that is usually less work than handling a breaking change (source: chapter-05-growing-pains.md).

### 3. Give consumers time to migrate

Two ways to support the old contract while the new one rolls out (source: chapter-05-growing-pains.md):

- **Coexist two versions of the service**, each exposing one contract. Costs: more infrastructure, data compatibility between versions, bug fixes against multiple branches. Newman prefers this *only* for short coexistence windows.
- **One running version, both contracts** (e.g. two APIs on different ports). Newman's preferred approach. Pushes complexity into the service implementation but avoids the multi-deployment headaches. Some teams support three or more old contracts in the same service for years — not fun, but the right call when external consumers can't change.

Inside a single team, lock-step deployment of consumer and producer is acceptable as an occasional fallback — easier to coordinate when you control both sides — but should not become a habit.

## Treat consumers like customers

> "Treat consumers of your service like customers — and you should treat your customers well!" (source: chapter-05-growing-pains.md)

Whatever the mechanism, breaking changes need good communication with the people running the consuming services. You're inconveniencing them at best.

## The survival framing

Newman is unusually blunt about this pain point:

> "Small microservice organizations that don't sort this stuff out won't last long enough to become large microservice organizations." (source: chapter-05-growing-pains.md)

If accidental breakages keep hitting production and there's no managed mechanism for deliberate ones, the architecture becomes untenable.
